Research

Controlling light–matter interactions in atomically thin semiconductors.

These materials are only a few atomic layers thick, yet they interact strongly with light. I study their excitons, couple them to optical cavities to form polaritons, explore extreme confinement in plasmonic nanogaps, and use ultrafast laser pulses to drive nonlinear optical switching.

01 Exciton interactions

How do atomically thin layers change exciton interactions?

Excitons are bound electron–hole states created when a semiconductor absorbs light. In van der Waals heterostructures, stacking and electric fields let us tune where these states live and how strongly they interact.

I study interlayer and hybrid excitons in MoS₂ and related TMD systems, linking their microscopic character to nonlinear optical response.

02 Strong light–matter coupling

How can optical cavities strengthen light–matter coupling?

Inside an optical microcavity, excitons can hybridise with confined photons to form polaritons: states that combine properties of light and matter.

I use angle-resolved spectroscopy, ultrafast measurements and cavity design to study nonlinear effects in this regime, including strong-to-weak coupling transitions and polariton lasing.

03 Ultrafast switching

How fast can light switch these states?

Femtosecond laser pulses let me resolve how excitons and polaritons evolve and drive them far from equilibrium.

I use broadband pump–probe spectroscopy for low-energy optical switching. In TMD microcavities, this enabled reversible strong-to-weak coupling switching below 4 pJ per pulse.

04 Extreme optical confinement

What changes when light is confined to nanometre-scale gaps?

Plasmonic nanocavities confine light far below its wavelength, producing very large local fields in gaps only a few nanometres wide.

I use nanoparticle-on-mirror structures and nanoscale semiconductor emitters to study strong light–matter coupling and nonlinear optical response under extreme confinement.
